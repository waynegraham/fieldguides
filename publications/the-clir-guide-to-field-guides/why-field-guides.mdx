## Accessible. Useful. Sustainable.

The stewardship of information resources is a shared responsibility across different nations, organizations, professional roles, and economic sectors. Approaches to stewardship necessarily evolve over time as collections grow and as new technologies for accessing and interpreting information appear. The ongoing publication of professional literature that is timely, openly accessible, and easy to understand is vitally important for the work of information practitioners at all levels of experience and responsibility. Such literature can frame debates, build consensus, and outline practical steps toward improving the quality and authenticity of information available today and in the future.

Traditional models of publishing in library and information science, cultural heritage, and allied fields have grown expensive, exclusive, and slow, and each year fewer and fewer publishers have the capacity to sustain the operations necessary to deliver the breadth of content that information workers need to stay up-to-date. At the same time, the challenges that memory organizations face have become exceptionally complex, multifaceted, and long term, requiring a more systemic, coherent, and collaborative approach to addressing them. New alliances, affiliations, and partnerships external to local institutions are often essential. Field Guides respond to these changes by offering a platform for jointly authored, practically oriented, standalone publications that equip information workers with the information they need to support cultural legacy in a world that is less predictable, more global, and increasingly interdependent.

Recognizing the growing need for quality professional publications designed for the 21st century information and cultural heritage workplace, CLIR has launched the Field Guide series, adapting the metaphor of the natural history “field guide.” The term “field” comprehends two related notions. A traditional field guide takes you into a bounded domain — a habitat of observable phenomena. In this respect, each Guide concisely describes best practices, methodologies, tools, and strategies relevant to a framing topic or theme of immediate relevance to the contemporary working environments of libraries, archives, and museums. A major goal, weaving our skills, insight, and aspirations into a sustained network of coordinated effect, introduces a second meaning of “field”: opening new lines of inquiry into practice, a new discipline, an advancing way of working together in a durable system of cooperative understanding.
